Step 1 · Data Collection
Step 1.0 · Participants

👥Human Subjects

Recruit two groups with no overlap. Standardise age, sex, and education where possible. All participants complete the same four eye-tracking tasks in a single session.

Group A · Schizophrenia patients (SCZ) Group B · Healthy Controls (HC) Matched: age · sex · education Exclusions: neurological illness · diabetes · head injury
4 EYE-TRACKING TASKS
Task 1 · Free-Viewing

🖼️Free-Viewing

Subject freely scans 42 images (faces, landscapes, animals, environments) for 8 sec each. No instructions about where to look. Captures natural visual exploration patterns.

42 images · 8 sec each Faces · Landscapes · Animals No gaze instruction
Task 2 · Smooth Pursuit

↔️Smooth Pursuit

Subject tracks a moving 0.5° dot across the screen. Three subtasks at varying frequencies. Tests sensorimotor coordination and motion perception.

HS4 · horizontal · 0.4 Hz LS2 · Lissajous · 0.2 Hz LS4 · Lissajous · 0.4 Hz 20 sec per trial
Task 3 · Fixation Stability

⊕Fixation Stability

Subject holds gaze on a central stationary dot; a distracting target appears on the periphery. Tests saccadic inhibition and sustained attention under competition.

Single target condition Distracter at 1.43° or 2.86° Each trial × 2 repetitions
★ New Addition
Task 4 · Antisaccade (New)

🔀Antisaccade

A stimulus flashes at the screen edge; subject must suppress the reflexive look and instead move gaze to the mirror-opposite location. Directly probes prefrontal inhibitory control — a hallmark SCZ deficit.

Peripheral stimulus onset Voluntary gaze suppression Prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) probe

Step 3 · Machine Learning
Step 3 · Early Fusion ML Architecture
🔀

Early Fusion — Pool All 59 Features Into One Dataset

Rather than training separate models per task (late fusion) and combining their outputs, all 59 features from all 4 tasks are merged into a single flat feature vector per participant and fed simultaneously into one unified model. This allows the algorithm to learn cross-task interaction patterns that no single-task model could detect.

Step 3.1 · Model Architecture

🤖GBDT · XGBoost

Gradient-Boosted Decision Tree ensemble. Weak learners work sequentially — each tree corrects the residual error of the previous. Exceptionally effective on tabular biomarker data.

XGBoost (R / Python) Modified 5-fold cross-validation Hyperparameter tuning Anti-overfitting regularisation

Output & Clinical Utility

✦Model Outputs & Clinical Applications

Primary Output: A per-subject probability score (0–100) indicating likelihood of SCZ. Clinicians can use this alongside their own assessment rather than relying on a hard binary label alone.
Early Detection: Because abnormalities appear at first-episode (and even in the prodromal phase), the model can flag at-risk individuals before full clinical psychosis onset, supporting earlier intervention.
Trait Marker: Eye-movement abnormalities are present regardless of illness duration, medication status, or disease stage — making this a stable biological trait marker, not a state-dependent effect.
Feature Importance: GBDT outputs ranked feature importances, identifying which of the 59 variables are most diagnostic. Free-viewing scan path length and fixation dispersion rank highest; antisaccade error rate adds new discriminatory power.

Why Each Task Adds Unique Value to the Model

🖼️
Free-Viewing
Captures real-world visual exploration deficits. Restricted scanning (shorter scan paths, lower dispersion) is the strongest single discriminator of SCZ.
↔️
Smooth Pursuit
Measures sensorimotor prediction and visual motion processing. SCZ patients show worse gain, more corrective saccades, and higher positional error.
⊕
Fixation Stability
Tests saccadic inhibition under visual competition. Reveals DLPFC dysfunction — patients fail to suppress eye movements toward irrelevant distractors.
🔀
Antisaccade
The most targeted probe of voluntary prefrontal inhibition. SCZ patients make significantly more errors and have longer latencies — a well-replicated endophenotype.
